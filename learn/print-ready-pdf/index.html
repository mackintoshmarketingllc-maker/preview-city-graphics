How to Save a Print-Ready PDF (From Whatever You Design In)

A print-ready PDF is a file exported (not just saved) as a PDF with fonts embedded and bleed included, so it prints the way it looks on your screen.

Reviewed by Greg Mackintosh

A print-ready PDF is a file that's been exported — not just saved — with the fonts locked in and bleed included, so what prints matches what you see on screen. The exact steps differ depending on what you designed in, but the goal is the same everywhere.

What this means for your order: a file that looks perfect on your screen can still hit trouble at the printer if it wasn't exported the right way — a font can get substituted, bleed can be missing. Getting the export right the first time saves a round of back-and-forth.

Whatever you're working in — InDesign, Canva, Photoshop, or one of the popular word processors, like Microsoft's PowerPoint — the export step matters as much as the design itself. The universal principles are the same regardless of tool: correct document size, bleed built in if your design needs edge-to-edge color, fonts embedded, high-resolution images, then export to PDF rather than just saving. Here's what to check by tool.

Canva

Use Canva's PDF Print download option rather than the standard PDF, since that's the version built for printing rather than screen viewing. If your design needs color to run to the edge, Canva has a bleed setting to turn on before you export — see what bleed is if that's new to you.

Word and PowerPoint

In Word and PowerPoint, using "Save As" or "Export" and choosing PDF is the export step — Microsoft's own help documentation walks through this for its desktop apps. That export alone doesn't automatically make the file print-ready, though: these tools weren't built with bleed and trim in mind the way design software is, so it's still worth checking document size, bleed, fonts, image resolution, and color before you send the file, and confirming with your printer how they want edge-to-edge designs handled from these tools.

InDesign

InDesign has a "Package" feature that bundles your file together with its fonts and linked images into one folder — sometimes called a save-for-printer workflow — so nothing gets left behind or substituted when it reaches the printer.

What about AI-generated images?

This one deserves its own note. AI image generators typically output a raster image (a grid of pixels, the same kind a digital photo is) around 1024 to 2048 pixels on a side. That's plenty for something postcard-sized, but it can fall apart at poster size — the same information gets stretched over a lot more paper. If you're planning to print an AI-generated image large, generate it at the biggest size the tool allows, or run it through an upscaling tool first, before you build your final file around it.

If you're not sure whether your image will hold up at the size you want, a proof strip — a small printed sample at the actual size — is the honest way to check, for a modest upcharge. See resolution and DPI in plain English for more on how that works.

One more thing worth knowing: the U.S. Supreme Court declined to review the question in March 2026, leaving the existing federal appellate ruling in place (Thaler v. Perlmutter) — purely AI-generated output with no human authorship isn't eligible for copyright, meaning nobody "owns" a pure AI image in the traditional sense. It's still usable under most platforms' commercial terms, but it likely won't be protectable as your own work unless you've made meaningful human edits to it. This is a legal area that can shift, so treat it as a starting point, not the final word.

What this article can't tell you

Export menus move — software updates change button names and locations regularly. This article describes the concept (embed fonts, include bleed, export to PDF) rather than an exact click path, since the exact steps will drift out of date faster than the underlying idea will. Your printer's accepted file types can also vary — some take native files, most want PDF only. Confirm with your shop before you export. Using a tool we don't cover here? The universal checklist above still applies — pair it with your tool's own export documentation.
