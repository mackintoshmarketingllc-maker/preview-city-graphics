Resolution and DPI, in Plain English

Resolution is how much visual information is packed into your image. A small file has a small amount of information — stretch it out to print bigger, and that same information has to cover more space, which is what makes it look blurry.

Reviewed by Greg Mackintosh

Resolution is how much visual information is packed into your image. A tiny file has a small amount of information — and when you stretch it out to print at a bigger size, that same information gets spread thinner, which is what makes it look blurry.

What this means for your order: an image can look perfectly sharp on your phone and still not have enough information to print clean at a bigger size. That's not a flaw in the photo — it's math. More paper to cover means the same information has to stretch further.

Why does a good-looking photo turn blurry when I print it big?

Think of it this way: you've got a small file with a small amount of information on it. It's shrunk down. When you expand it, you're taking that shrunk-down information and stretching it out — so it gets blurry, and that's not what you want. For the file to look good at a bigger size, it needs to be a bigger file to begin with.

That's the whole concept behind "resolution" or DPI (dots per inch): it's a measure of how much detail is packed into each inch of the final print. Stretch the same amount of detail over more inches, and each inch gets less of it. Professional print work generally aims for somewhere in the 150–300 DPI range, and you can see the tradeoff directly: an image that's 300 DPI at 2x2 inches drops to 150 DPI once it's enlarged to 4x4 inches. Same amount of information, twice the space to cover.

What's the most common way this trips people up?

Two things, almost every time. First, the image doesn't match the shape (the aspect ratio) of the size someone wants to print — say, a photo shaped for a square post that someone wants to turn into a 4x6 postcard. Second, and probably the single most common issue: someone downloads a low-resolution image off the internet — a PNG made for a web page — and tries to turn it into a poster. Resolution is likely the single biggest reason a finished print doesn't come out looking the way someone hoped.

What should I do differently when I take a photo on my phone?

Save it at full size. Phones already capture a lot of detail, and starting from that full-size original gives you the most to work with when it comes time to print.

What if I'm not sure whether my image is high-res enough?

This is a fair question, and there's no single formula that works for every job. Rather than guess, the most reliable way to know for sure is a proof strip: a small printed sample of your image at the actual size it'll print at, for a modest upcharge. You get to hold the real thing in your hand and judge it yourself before committing to the full run.

What this article can't tell you

There's no universal DPI number that guarantees a "good" print — it depends on the image and the size you're printing at. This article can tell you why resolution matters and what to watch for; it can't tell you, sight unseen, whether your specific file will hold up at your specific size. That's exactly what a proof strip is for.
